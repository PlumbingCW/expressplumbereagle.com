When a sump pump failure is an emergency

A failed pump matters when water is rising in the pit, in the crawlspace or at the lowest floor. A pump that simply runs a lot after rain is working. A pump that hums with no water leaving, or a pit that fills and sits still, is not.

Sump pump or sewage ejector: which one do you have?

A sump pump moves groundwater or rain that collects in a pit and sends it outside. A sewage ejector pump lifts wastewater from a below-grade bathroom or laundry up to the main drain. They look alike, but they carry different loads.

How the two pumps differ.
Pump. What it moves. Where it sits. Warning sign.
Sump pump. Groundwater and rain. Pit in the floor or crawlspace. Pit fills, pump runs nonstop.
Sewage ejector. Toilet and drain waste. Sealed basin, tight lid. Gurgling, sewer smell, slow flush.
Backup pump. Same as the main pump. Beside the primary pump. Alarm sounds or battery is low.

Because an ejector pushes sewage into your home's sewer line, check which district serves your address, since the Eagle Sewer District provides sewer service in Eagle. We also treat an ejector failure more carefully. The lid stays sealed, and you should keep people and pets away from any overflow.

Why sump pumps stop working

Most failures trace to one of a handful of parts. We work through them in order, starting with the easiest to check.

  • No power: a tripped breaker, a dead outlet or an unplugged cord.
  • Stuck float switch: it jams against the pit wall or a pipe and never signals the pump to start.
  • Failed check valve: water falls back into the pit and the pump cycles constantly.
  • Clogged intake: gravel, silt or debris blocks the screen.
  • Worn motor: the pump hums, gets hot, or runs with no flow.

If the pump is old or the motor is burned out, replacement is usually the better call than a repair. A pump with a bad switch and a bad bearing is a pump near the end of its life.

Testing and keeping a pump ready

  1. Pour water in.

    Add a bucket of water to the pit slowly and watch the float rise.

  2. Listen for the start.

    The pump should start before the water reaches the top and drain the pit.

  3. Check discharge.

    Walk the outside end of the discharge pipe to make sure water leaves and drains away from the house.

  4. Check the check valve.

    After the pump stops, water should not fall back into the pit.

Run the test before the wet season and after any big storm. A battery backup is worth asking about if the power tends to drop in storms. See our guide to water line repair if the wet area is in the yard instead of the pit.

What drives the cost of sump pump repair

What shapes the price.

  • Repair or replacement. A float switch or check valve is a small repair. A burned motor usually means a new pump.
  • Pump type and size. Sump pumps, sewage ejectors and battery backups use different equipment and horsepower.
  • Pit and discharge condition. Silt in the pit or a frozen or damaged discharge line adds work.
  • Access. A pit in a tight crawlspace takes longer than one in an open utility room.
  • Time of the call. After-hours and weekend calls are priced with the schedule in mind.

Many homeowners policies exclude water that backs up through a sump or drain unless you added that coverage, and they often exclude wear and the pump itself. Ask your agent specifically about sump backup before the wet season.

Do you handle sump pump repair emergencies at night or on weekends in Eagle?

Why is my sump pump running all the time?

A stuck float or a failed check valve are common causes, and so is heavy rain or a high water table. We check the float and valve first, then the pit inflow.

Does every home in Eagle, ID need a sump pump?

Can you replace a sewage ejector pump?

Yes. We pull the old unit, check the basin and check valve, and set the new pump with a sealed lid.
