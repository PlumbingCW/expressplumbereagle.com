What Size Tankless Water Heater Does an Eagle, ID Home Need?

Written by Eagle Express Plumbing. Published October 15, 2026.

A tankless water heater is sized by two numbers, flow rate in gallons per minute and temperature rise in degrees. The unit has to deliver the most hot water you run at one time at the coldest incoming water you see. The U.S. Department of Energy says tankless output is limited to about 2 to 5 gallons per minute, so a busy household in Eagle, ID may need more than one unit or a different plan.

This guide walks through the math, the gas and electric limits and the winter problem that catches people out.

Flow rate and temperature rise: the two numbers that decide everything

Flow rate is how much hot water you want at once, measured in gallons per minute. Temperature rise is the gap between the cold water coming in and the hot water you want out. A heater that makes a 50 degree rise at 5 gallons per minute may only make a 70 degree rise at 3.

That is why a single number on the box means little. Every tankless maker publishes a chart of flow against rise. You read the chart at your needed rise and see how many gallons per minute you actually get.

Think of it like a garden hose through a heater. Push more water through, and each gallon spends less time being warmed. The Department of Energy's 2 to 5 gallons per minute ceiling is the practical limit of the whole class.

A sizing worksheet you can fill in at the kitchen table

You do not need special tools. You need a pencil and the model numbers of your fixtures.

  1. List every fixture that could run at the same time, such as two showers and a kitchen faucet.
  2. Find each fixture's flow rating. It is often stamped on the showerhead or faucet, or listed in the maker's data.
  3. Add the flows for the worst realistic moment in your day. That total is your peak gallons per minute.
  4. Decide the hot water temperature you want at the tap, then subtract the cold inlet temperature to get your rise.
  5. Compare your peak flow and rise against the maker's chart for each model you are considering.

Do not size for the weekday morning only. Size for the busiest realistic hour, such as guests in town, because that is the day the heater gets judged.

Simultaneous use: how your habits change the answer

Two people showering and a dishwasher running is a different load than one person showering. The table below shows how common habits push on the heater and what to do about it.

Household habits and sizing moves.
Habit. What it asks of the heater. Sizing move.
One shower at a time. Low peak flow. A mid-size unit often fits.
Two showers at once. Peak flow doubles. Larger unit or two units.
Shower plus dishwasher or washer. Mixed high and sustained draws. Add the flows, then check the chart.
Long soaking tub fill. High flow for several minutes. Check the unit's cap at your rise.
Guests every weekend. Peak load repeats. Size for the guest day.

If the numbers do not fit in one unit, there are three ways out. Stagger the use, install two units, or consider a storage tank heater installation that can cover short bursts better.

Why winter water makes tankless sizing harder

Incoming water is colder in winter, so the temperature rise you need is bigger. A bigger rise means the same heater delivers fewer gallons per minute. A unit that feels generous in September may run short in January.

We do not give an Eagle, ID winter inlet temperature here, because it varies with how deep your line sits and how long the water has been in it. A plumber can measure your incoming temperature on a cold morning. Size from that reading, not from a guess.

Gas input, gas line size and venting

A gas tankless heater burns a lot of fuel in a short time. Its input is rated in thousands of Btu per hour, and the house gas line, the meter and the regulator must all be able to deliver it. The pipe that fed a small tank heater is often too small for a big tankless unit.

Venting is the other limit. Tankless heaters use sealed or power-vented exhaust, and the vent type, length and number of bends all matter. A plumber checks the run against the maker's instructions before choosing the model.

  • Read the unit's input rating and compare it to the line size and meter capacity.
  • Confirm the vent type and the longest allowed run.
  • Find a clear route for the vent and the condensate drain.
  • Plan for service space around the unit, so valves and filters are reachable.

Electric tankless units and the electrical panel

Electric tankless heaters need heavy current. In general terms, a unit sized for a whole house can draw far more amperage than a typical tank heater, and it may need its own heavy circuit or even a panel upgrade. Check the amperage on the data plate against your panel before you commit to a model.

Small point-of-use electric units are a different case. They serve a single sink or shower and draw less. They are not a whole-house answer, but they can cover a faraway bathroom.

When one unit is not enough

Two tankless units can be linked to share the load. A tankless heater paired with a small storage tank is another layout. Both cost more to install than a single unit, so they make sense only when your peak flow truly calls for them.

Common questions

What size tankless water heater do I need for a family of four?

It depends on how many fixtures run together and on your winter inlet temperature, not on family size alone. Add the flow of the fixtures you use at once, find the rise you need, and compare that to the maker's chart.

Why does my tankless heater run out of hot water when two showers run?

The unit hit its flow limit at your temperature rise. The Department of Energy puts tankless output at about 2 to 5 gallons per minute, and two showers can exceed what one unit gives in winter.

Does an electric tankless heater need a new electrical panel?

Often it needs a heavy dedicated circuit, and sometimes the panel itself must be upgraded. Check the unit's amperage against your panel before you buy.

Can I use my existing gas line for a tankless water heater?

Only if it can carry the unit's input rating. A plumber checks the pipe size and the meter capacity first. If the line is too small, it must be upsized.

What should I ask my water utility in Eagle, ID before buying a tankless heater?

Ask for its yearly water quality report, since scale affects a tankless heat exchanger. Which supplier serves you, whether Veolia, the city or a private well, depends on your address.

Do I have to descale a tankless water heater?

Many makers call for periodic descaling, and the interval depends on the model and your water. Follow the manual that came with your unit.
