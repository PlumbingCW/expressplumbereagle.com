High Water Bill in Eagle, ID: How to Check for a Hidden Leak

Written by Eagle Express Plumbing. Published October 15, 2026.

A high water bill with no wet spot in sight usually points to a hidden leak, a running toilet, or a sprinkler system. In Eagle, ID you can check the first two in about two hours with your water meter and a few drops of food coloring.

Start with the meter. If it moves while every tap is off, water is going somewhere it should not, and the rest of this guide helps you find where.

Read the bill for gallons, not just the total

Look at the usage line, not the amount owed. Compare the gallons used this month with the same month last year. A jump in gallons means water went through the meter, which rules out a rate change or a billing fee as the whole story.

For scale, the EPA says a family of four using more than 12,000 gallons a month in winter may have serious leaks. Summer use runs higher because of watering, so compare like seasons.

The two-hour meter test

  1. Turn everything off.

    Shut off taps, the dishwasher, the washing machine and the ice maker. Make sure no one flushes a toilet.

  2. Read the meter.

    Find the meter box, usually near the street or curb. Lift the lid and write down the numbers, or take a photo.

  3. Wait two hours.

    Use no water at all while you wait.

  4. Read it again.

    If the numbers moved, you probably have a leak, according to the EPA. Some meters also have a small dial or triangle that spins when water is flowing.

If the meter did not move, the leak may be in an irrigation zone that is on a timer, or the high use may be real. If it moved, go on to the next tests.

The toilet dye test

Toilets are the most common hidden leak, because the flapper can leak into the bowl without making a sound. Put a few drops of food coloring in the tank, and do not flush. Wait 10 minutes.

If color shows up in the bowl, the flapper or fill valve is leaking. Flush afterward so the dye does not stain. A toilet that runs all night can use a surprising amount of water, and the fix is usually a small part.

  • Do the dye test on every toilet in the house.
  • Look for a faint trickle sound at the tank.
  • Check for drips at faucets and under sinks.
  • Check hose bibs and the water heater drain for slow drips.

Numbers that help you judge how big the leak is

The EPA says the average household wastes more than 9,300 gallons of water a year from leaks. About 9 percent of homes leak 50 gallons or more per day, and fixing easy leaks can cut a bill by about 10 percent.

A faucet that drips once per second wastes more than 3,000 gallons a year. A small drip is still worth fixing, though a big jump in the bill usually means something larger.

Irrigation or indoors? Splitting the bill

Sprinklers can use more water than the rest of the house combined in summer. A stuck valve, a broken head or a cracked line can run for days before anyone notices, especially if the zone is out of sight.

Neighborhoods like The Shores and Banbury Meadows have pressurized irrigation, which is a second pipe network for sprinklers. Two Rivers homes sit between the channels of the Boise River and also run pressurized irrigation. Ask whether your sprinklers run on that system or on your house water, because only house water shows up on the household meter you are testing.

If your home has a separate irrigation line or meter, read it apart from the indoor meter. If you only have one, shut off the irrigation controller and the supply to it, then repeat the meter test. A leak detection visit can use listening gear and pressure tests to separate the two.

When the leak is between the meter and the house

A wet yard patch, a green strip of grass in dry weather, or sudden low pressure can mean the pipe from the meter to the house is leaking. Who owns which stretch depends on where the utility's responsibility ends, so ask your utility.

Do not dig on a guess. Call 811 first, then have the leak located so the dig is short. When it is time to repair the pipe, our water line repair page lays out how that work goes.

Common questions

How do I know if I have a hidden water leak?

Use the EPA meter test. Read the meter, use no water for two hours, and read it again. If the numbers moved, you probably have a leak.

Why is my water bill high when nothing looks wet?

A running toilet, a leaking irrigation zone, or a slab or yard line can all leak out of sight. Check the meter and do the dye test before you assume the bill is wrong.

How much water is too much in winter?

The EPA says a family of four using more than 12,000 gallons a month in winter may have serious leaks. Your household may differ, so compare with your own past bills.

Can a running toilet really raise a bill that much?

Yes, because it can run all day and night with no sound. The dye test takes 10 minutes and catches most of them.

Who do I call about a high water bill in Eagle, ID, my plumber or Veolia?

Both, in order. Call the supplier on your bill, which may be Veolia Water Idaho, the City of Eagle, or a district, to check the meter and usage. If the meter shows a leak, call us at (208) 679-8357.

Do landlords or property managers deal with this?

Yes. Property managers call us when a tenant's bill spikes, and the meter test tells them whether the leak is inside a unit or in the shared line.
